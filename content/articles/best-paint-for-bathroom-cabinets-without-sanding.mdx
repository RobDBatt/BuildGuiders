## What "without sanding" actually means

Start with the honest version, because it will save you a repaint. No paint bonds to a slick factory finish on its own. What the good products do is replace the sanding with adhesion chemistry, and that only works if the surface is clean and dull first. Skipping the sandpaper is realistic. Skipping the prep is not.

The prep that does replace sanding is two steps and about an hour on a normal vanity. Degrease with a household cleaner or TSP substitute — bathroom cabinet doors carry hairspray, hand lotion and aerosol residue, and paint will not stick to any of it. Then wipe the doors down with a liquid deglosser such as Krud Kutter Gloss-Off, which etches the sheen chemically instead of mechanically. A surface that has gone from shiny to dull under a rag is what these paints are asking for when the can says no sanding required.

After that the question is what you put on. There are two routes. Use a paint formulated to bond to gloss directly, or use an ordinary cabinet enamel over a bonding primer — INSL-X STIX is the usual one, and it grips surfaces that have no business holding paint. The one-product route is less work. The primer route gives you any colour in any brand.

The spec that matters after adhesion is hardness, and specifically block resistance: whether a painted door sticks to a painted frame the first time you close it. Wall paint has no block resistance at all. That is the failure you notice on day two, when the cabinet opens with a tearing sound and takes some of the finish with it.

Sheen should be satin or semi-gloss. Satin hides the imperfections in an old door; semi-gloss wipes cleaner and reads more like a factory finish. Anything flatter than satin is not a cabinet.

## Sherwin-Williams Emerald Urethane Trim Enamel

This is the upgrade if the cabinet gets hammered — a family bathroom, a vanity that doubles as a step stool, doors that get pushed shut with a wet hand ten times a day. It is a waterborne alkyd urethane, which means latex cleanup and oil-based hardness, and it cures to the hardest, most block-resistant film on this page.

It also lays down beautifully. Flow and levelling are the reason people pay for it: brush marks sink out, and a careful hand gets something close to sprayed. Satin, semi-gloss and gloss, and the full Sherwin-Williams colour range, which Cabinet Coat cannot match.

The catch is that it is not a no-sanding product. Sherwin-Williams expects a properly prepared surface, and on a glossy factory finish that means the STIX bonding primer first. Two products, one extra coat, and the result is better than anything else here.

## Benjamin Moore Advance

Advance gets picked for the same reason as Emerald Urethane — waterborne alkyd, furniture-hard, levels like oil — and it comes in the Benjamin Moore fan deck, which is the deciding factor if you are matching an existing colour in the house.

What separates it is the schedule. Advance wants 16 hours between coats, and it keeps hardening for several days after that. On a two-coat job that is a weekend where the doors stay off the hinges on sawhorses, not an afternoon. Rush it and you will press fingerprints into a finish that felt dry.

It needs the bonding primer on gloss, same as Emerald Urethane. Choose it over the Sherwin-Williams if you want a specific Benjamin Moore colour and you have the days; choose Emerald Urethane if you want to be done sooner.

## What to skip

Any general-purpose interior latex, including the good ones. Emerald and Aura are excellent wall paints and they have no business on a cabinet — no block resistance, no hardness, and they will mark under a fingernail.

Skip chalk paint unless you are committed to a wax or topcoat over it, and understand that a waxed finish in a bathroom is a maintenance job rather than a finish.

And skip spray cans for a whole vanity. The coverage per can makes it expensive fast, the film is thin, and a run in an aerosol finish is very hard to fix without going back to bare.
