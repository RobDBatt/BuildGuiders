The wall is dry and it looks striped. Raised ridge lines at the edge of every roller pass, darker lanes where passes overlapped, maybe a few dull patches that don't match the sheen around them. Bad news first: there's no fix that skips repainting. Anyone promising you can buff out dried roller marks is selling something. The good news is the redo is one light sand and one coat, and if you change three things about how you roll, the marks won't come back.

## The fix, in order

1. **Let it dry completely.** Overnight for latex. Sanding tacky paint smears it into a bigger mess than you started with.
2. **Sand the ridges flat.** 220-grit paper on a sanding pole, light pressure, just enough to knock down the raised lines and edges. You're leveling the surface, not stripping it. Run a bare hand over it; smooth is done.
3. **Wipe off the dust** with a barely damp rag and let the wall dry.
4. **Recoat the whole wall, corner to corner.** Not just the streaked spots. Touch-up patches flash under raking light, and anything with sheen (eggshell on up) makes the flashing worse. One full coat with good technique erases everything underneath.

That's the repair. Skip step 2 and the ridges telegraph straight through the new coat.

## Why it happened

Three suspects, and it's usually more than one of them.

**The cover.** Bargain covers mat down halfway through a wall and start dragging stripes. On smooth drywall, use a quality 3/8 in. nap woven cover; the Purdy White Dove is the standard answer for a reason. Save 1/2 in. nap for light texture, and skip the foam rollers entirely on walls.

**The load.** A starved roller drags and leaves dry, patchy streaks. An overloaded one squeezes paint out at the ends of the cover and lays down the fat ridge lines painters call rope marks. Load the cover evenly in the tray and reload every few square feet, before it runs dry, not after.

**The clock.** Lap marks are wet paint applied over half-dried paint. Latex starts tacking up in minutes on a warm, dry wall. Work in sections about three feet wide, keep a wet edge, and never re-roll an area that's started to set. Missed a thin spot? Leave it. Catch it on the next coat.

Pressure belongs on this list too. Pressing harder doesn't spread paint, it squeezes it out the ends of the cover and carves ridges. Let the nap carry the paint.

## Technique for the recoat

Cut in the edges with a brush first, then roll while the cut-in is still wet where you can. Fill each section with a loose W, then fill in the gaps. Finish every section with laydown passes: nearly weightless top-to-bottom strokes in one direction, each overlapping the last by half a roller width. That final pass is the single biggest difference between a wall that looks rolled and a wall that looks sprayed.

Paint quality matters more than people want to hear. Builder-grade flat shows every lap and every touch-up. A better acrylic levels as it dries and hides the small stuff. It also self-primes, so the sanded ridges from step 2 disappear under one coat instead of two.

## Know when it's not you

If streaks reappear even with a good cover and a wet edge, look at conditions. A wall in direct sun or a room with a fan blasting dries too fast for any technique to keep up; paint when the wall is shaded and kill the airflow until the coat sets. Ancient paint that's been in the garage through a few winters doesn't level right either. And if the old wall color is bleeding through in lanes, that's a coverage problem, not a roller problem: you need another full coat, not harder rolling.
