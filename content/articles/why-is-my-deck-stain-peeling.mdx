A deck stain that peels inside its first year or two almost always peeled for one reason: it sat on top of the wood as a film instead of soaking in. Penetrating stain wears away by fading. Only a film can lift off in flakes. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory's [*Wood Handbook*](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/62269) describes exactly this failure. If a semi-transparent finish still looks shiny an hour after it goes on, it has not penetrated, and that thin surface film "may crack and peel within a year or two." Behr's label for its [Premium Semi-Transparent Waterproofing Stain & Sealer](https://www.behr.com/products/wood-stains/behr-premium-semi-transparent-waterproofing-stain-and-sealer) says the same thing in plainer words: over-application "will lead to surface failure including peeling and cracking."

So the question is not really why stain peels. It is how a film got there on your deck, and the flakes will usually tell you.

## Check the peeling first

Two minutes with a putty knife sorts out which of four causes you have. Look at where it is peeling and at the back of a flake.

- **Glossy, thicker patches lifting in low spots, board centres and where the applicator overlapped:** too much stain, left to dry on the surface (cause 1).
- **Flakes with wood fibres stuck to the back:** stain went onto weathered, degraded wood (cause 2).
- **The whole deck peeling evenly, after solid stain, paint or a clear film finish:** a film-forming product on a horizontal deck (cause 3).
- **Peeling worst near the ground, in shade, or where water sits after rain:** moisture getting into the wood under the finish (cause 4).

More than one can be true at once. The fix is the same for all of them in the end, but knowing the cause stops it happening again.

## Cause 1: too much stain

This is the failure both sources above describe. The wood takes in what it can absorb, and the rest dries on top as a film. On a deck floor that film cracks under sun and foot traffic, and water gets in behind it. The *Wood Handbook* also warns that even on weathered wood a second coat of some semi-transparent stains may not absorb at all, which is how a deck that looked fine after coat one ends up peeling a year after coat two.

This does not mean one coat is always right. Behr's semi-transparent stain & sealer calls for two thin coats, at 250 to 300 square feet per gallon for the first and 500 to 600 for the second. Follow your own can. The test is the same either way: if the surface is still shiny an hour later, wipe the excess off before it dries.

## Cause 2: stain over weathered wood

Grey, sun-worn wood has a loose surface layer of degraded fibres. Stain bonds to that layer, and the layer lets go of the board. The *Wood Handbook* notes that when a finish fails on weathered wood, wood fibres are often attached to the back of the film. Fibres on the back of your flakes mean the deck needed cleaning or sanding before it was stained.

## Cause 3: a film-forming product on a deck

Solid stain, deck paint, and clear film finishes all form a skin on the surface, and decks are hard on skins. The *Wood Handbook* says it is "extremely challenging" to get film-forming finishes to adhere to decks, that peeling is very common, and that solid-colour stains form films and so peel just like paint. It recommends penetrating finishes for decks instead.

If the whole deck is peeling evenly and it was coated with one of these, the product was the problem rather than the application.

## Cause 4: moisture in the wood

The *Wood Handbook* calls keeping wood at or below about 20 percent moisture content in service the single most important step for any finish to perform. It says plainly that "nothing will coat wet wood well." Two ways this goes wrong on a deck:

- **The wood was wet when you stained it.** New pressure-treated lumber is sometimes still saturated when it arrives. The *Wood Handbook* says you can tell because it is exceptionally heavy, or water comes out when you squeeze a small piece. Behr's label requires a completely dry surface before application.
- **The wood gets wet and stays wet.** Peeling concentrated near the ground, in permanent shade, or where water sits after rain points at drainage, not at the stain.

## How to fix peeling deck stain

Start with the least drastic option that matches what you found.

1. **A few small flaking patches on an otherwise sound penetrating stain.** Scrape off everything loose, clean the patch, let it dry, and spot-coat thinly with the same stain. Penetrating finishes are easy to touch up for exactly this reason. This doesn't work over a film, though: new stain on top of a peeling film just peels with it.
2. **Peeling across large areas, or any peeling film.** The old coat has to come off before anything new goes on. Apply a deck stain stripper and keep it wet for the dwell time on its label. Scrape, then rinse at low pressure with a fan tip. The *Wood Handbook* warns that power washing erodes the softer earlywood faster than the latewood and leaves ridges, and low-pressure washing does less damage.
3. **Clean and brighten.** Follow the stripper maker's instructions on what comes next. Behr, for one, says its No. 63 cleaner must follow its No. 64 stripper to clean, brighten and neutralize the wood.
4. **Let it dry completely.** This is the step that gets rushed, and the one cause 4 is about.
5. **Recoat thinly with a penetrating stain.** Stay inside the temperature range on the can. Behr's semi-transparent stain & sealer must go on between 40 and 90°F, with no rain expected for 24 hours. The *Wood Handbook* advises limiting stain to what the surface can absorb and names a brush as the best tool, because rollers and sprayers can put too much on a horizontal surface. Back-brush, and wipe anything still shiny an hour later.
6. **Solid stain or paint that peeled.** Stripping gets you partway, and the flat boards usually need sanding to finish the job. Then decide whether to go back to a solid colour, accepting it will need the same care next time, or switch to a penetrating stain.

## Does the warranty cover it?

Read it before you assume so. Behr's warranty for its semi-transparent stain & sealer runs 6 years on horizontal surfaces. It is "not valid when the product is not properly applied to a properly prepared surface," and Behr's own label treats over-application as a cause of failure. So even a successful claim leaves the strip-and-recoat weekend to you.

## Stop it happening again

Every cause above comes back to the same two rules: only put on what the wood can absorb, and only put it on clean, dry wood. Get those right and the stain wears by fading, which is the failure you want.
