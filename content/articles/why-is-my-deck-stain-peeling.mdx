Peeling deck stain almost always traces back to one of four things, and they rank in a clear order of how often they're the real cause: bad prep, over-application, wood that was too wet, or the wrong product. Diagnose which one you've got and the fix follows directly. Here's what the failure actually tells you.

## Cause 1: the surface wasn't clean or bare enough

This is the top cause by a wide margin. Stain has to bond to the wood, and it can't do that through a layer of dirt, mildew, mill glaze, or an old finish. Trap any of that under a fresh coat and the new stain bonds to the contaminant instead of the wood, then sheets off. If your stain is peeling in sheets or flakes rather than fading evenly, poor prep is your prime suspect.

The tell: peel a flake and look at the back. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory's [*Wood Handbook*](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/62269) notes that when a finish fails on weathered wood, wood fibers are often attached to the back of the film. That points at a degraded surface that needed cleaning or sanding before the stain went on.

## Cause 2: too much product

Stain is not paint, and more coats do not equal more protection. [Semi-transparent and penetrating stains](/guides/best-semi-transparent-deck-stain) are designed to soak in, and the wood can only absorb so much. Anything past that sits on the surface as a film, and films on a horizontal, sun-baked, foot-trafficked deck floor crack and peel. A second heavy coat applied before the first soaked in, or stain left to puddle and dry on top, builds exactly the film that fails.

The tell: peeling concentrated in low spots and board centers where excess pooled.

## Cause 3: the wood was too wet

Moisture is the quiet killer. Stain applied over damp wood, or over a deck that gets soaked and can't dry because of poor drainage or tight board spacing, never bonds properly. Trapped moisture also drives swelling and shrinking cycles that shear the stain off the surface. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory's *Wood Handbook* puts it bluntly: nothing will coat wet wood well. It calls keeping wood at or below about 20 percent moisture content in service the single most important step for a finish to perform. Behr's label for its semi-transparent stain & sealer says the surface must be completely dry before application.

## Cause 4: the wrong product for an exterior deck floor

Interior or film-forming finishes belong nowhere near a deck. Polyurethane and indoor wood finishes form a hard surface film with no UV flexibility, so on an exterior deck they crack and peel fast. Decks need coatings built for horizontal exterior wood. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory's *Wood Handbook* says it is extremely challenging to get film-forming finishes to adhere to decks, that peeling is very common, and recommends penetrating finishes instead.

## How to fix it, step by step

There's no shortcut here. A peeling finish has to come off before anything new goes on.

1. Strip the failed coating. Apply a chemical deck stain stripper to soften and lift the peeling film, then scrape and pressure wash it back toward bare wood. Spot-sand any stubborn glossy patches.

2. Clean and brighten. Wash with a deck cleaner to pull remaining gray and mildew (the *Wood Handbook* notes that sodium percarbonate, an oxygen bleach, is the active ingredient in many of them), rinse, then brighten. Follow the stripper maker's instructions here: Behr, for one, says its No. 63 cleaner must follow its No. 64 stripper to clean, brighten and neutralize the wood.

3. Let it dry completely. Behr's label requires a completely dry surface before application, and most others say the same. Don't rush this step.

4. Apply thin coats, correctly. Stain in the shade, not direct sun, inside the temperature range on your can (Behr's [Premium Semi-Transparent Waterproofing Stain & Sealer](https://www.behr.com/products/wood-stains/behr-premium-semi-transparent-waterproofing-stain-and-sealer) must go on between 40 and 90°F with no rain expected for 24 hours). The *Wood Handbook* advises limiting the stain to what the surface can absorb and names a brush as the best tool, because rollers and sprayers can put too much on a horizontal surface. Back-brush, wipe any excess, and use the spread rate printed on the can.

## Stop it from happening again

The pattern is consistent: prep fully, never over-apply, stain only dry wood, and use a penetrating product made for decks. Get those four right and the finish wears by fading instead of peeling, which is exactly what you want.
